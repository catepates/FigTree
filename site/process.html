The process

How Cate works

Each piece is made by hand, fired twice and finished with glazes Cate mixes herself, drawing on more than 30 years of working with clay. It takes weeks, a lot of heat and some careful chemistry to get from a block of clay to a finished fox or vessel.

Draft, hidden from the menu and search. Cate’s quotes are from her August interview on The Hutt Zone (Access Radio). Placeholders get replaced after Thursday’s photos and recording.

Step 1

The clay

Cate works in earthenware, from New Zealand clay makers like Mac’s Mud and Primo.

“The clay here is pretty awesome, actually. It does what I need it to.”

To add from Thursday: which clay she uses most, and why.

Step 2

Building by hand

Nothing is thrown on a wheel. Vessels are slab-built: the clay is rolled out flat, then cut and joined by hand. The foxes, hares, owls and ravens are modelled by hand, piece by piece. Texture goes in while the clay is still soft.

“I get a chance to put a lot of surface decoration into the piece while I’m making it, but also afterwards.”

Step 3

Drying

Every piece has to dry slowly and evenly before it can go near a kiln. Rush it, and it cracks.

To add from Thursday: how long pieces dry for.

Photo to come: the kiln, with the controller showing the temperature

Step 4

The first firing

The dry clay goes into the kiln and is taken up to about 1000°C. This bisque firing changes the clay for good, from something that would dissolve back into mud into hard ceramic.

“It chemically transforms into a hard surface, which will then be able to take the glaze.”

To add from Thursday: how many hours the firing and cooling take.

Step 5

Drawing

On the bisqued surface Cate draws with ceramic pencil: birds, animals, flowers and lines of her own poetry, inspired by the forest she walks in most days.

Photo to come: Cate mixing glaze in mask and gloves, raw materials and scales

Step 6

Mixing the glazes

Cate makes her glazes from scratch, weighing out raw minerals and oxides to her own recipes. As dry powders some of these materials are hazardous to breathe, so the mixing happens in her garage workshop with a respirator, gloves and careful clean-up.

“I paint on glazes that I’ve mixed up in my garage with all my heavy duty masks and gear.”

To add from Thursday: what goes into a glaze, how she works out a recipe, and which materials need the most care.

Step 7

The glaze firing

Glazed pieces go back into the kiln, this time to about 1100°C. The glaze melts and fuses to the clay as the deep blues, greens and honeys of the finished work. Every opening of the kiln is a moment of truth: a glaze can run, or a piece can crack, and no two firings come out quite the same.

“The glaze melts and forms the lovely shiny surface.”
